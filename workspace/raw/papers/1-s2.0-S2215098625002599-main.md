#### ABSTRACT

This study presents a design methodology for synchronous reluctance (SynRM) and permanent magnet-assisted synchronous reluctance motors (PMaSynRM), developed through rotor lamination optimizations without altering the existing stator platform. This approach enables the realization of different motor types using a common stator, eliminating the need for additional tooling costs in the production line. It is based on the principle of "Efficiency Class Upgrade with Minimum Die Cost", whereby the existing tooling infrastructure is preserved, and higher-efficiency motors are prototyped at low cost through topological modifications in the rotor design.

Using a multi-objective genetic algorithm (MOGA), rotor designs were optimized to achieve Pareto-optimal solutions among conflicting targets such as torque production, efficiency, magnet volume, and torque ripple. The electromagnetic performance of rotor geometries was assessed via 2D finite element analysis (FEA), providing flux distribution, torque profiles, and efficiency curves.

A reference IE2-class induction motor (IM) was used as the baseline, upon which SynRM and PMaSynRM models were designed using the same stator. These three motor types were comparatively analyzed, along with a dimensional-level comparison against the IE4 version of the same motor. Throughout the process, the stator geometry, slot structure, and winding features remained unchanged, ensuring design novelty through rotor-side innovations.

The results demonstrate the electromagnetic potential and structural compatibility of SynRM and PMaSynRM configurations with the existing stator. The proposed method offers a scalable and cost-effective solution for manufacturers aiming to adopt high-efficiency motor technologies, paving a transition path from industrial motors to electric vehicle (EV) applications via a unified stator platform.

This study is limited to an IE2-class, frame-90, four-pole squirrel-cage IM stator reference; applying the methodology to other frame sizes and pole counts requires re-parameterization. From a practical standpoint, redesigning only the rotor eliminates stator/winding tooling costs, enabling efficiency-class upgrades and making the approach suitable for rapid industrial adoption. From a social perspective, higher efficiency and reduced dependence on rare-earth materials contribute to energy savings and supply-chain sustainability.

## **1. Introduction**

Electric motors used in industry today account for nearly 45 % of global electricity consumption. This significant share has increased the importance of efforts to improve motor efficiency. The efficiency classes defined by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) standardize energy performance and motivate manufacturers to develop higher-efficiency motors. However, achieving advanced efficiency classes like IE4 and IE5 is not always feasible with conventional squirrelcage IM, prompting growing interest in alternative motor topologies.

In the present industrial and global scenario, the necessity of studying alternative topologies has become more evident. The main reasons include increasingly strict energy efficiency regulations (IE4/ IE5), supply chain risks associated with rare-earth magnets, and the

At this point, a practical case relevant to this study is noteworthy: in the GAMAK production line, existing IE2-class IM stators have been preserved while only the rotor laminations were modified. Before proceeding with the detailed literature review, it is useful to briefly outline the main research areas addressed by the surveyed studies. These works can generally be grouped into four categories: (i) the influence of rotor barrier geometries on performance, (ii) electromagnetic design optimization methods, (iii) cost–performance trade-offs in industrial motor platforms, and (iv) material utilization strategies, including ferrite and rare-earth magnets. This framework clearly provides the reader with an overview of the focus areas of the subsequent literature review.

SynRM offer promising advantages with their magnet-free design-—providing benefits in cost, supply chain reliability, and energy efficiency. Early theoretical studies on SynRM focused on magnetic reaction-based analysis of multiphase synchronous machines [[1,2\]](#page-11-0). In the 1950s and 60s, research on rotor segmentation and saliency ratio laid foundational design principles [[3,4\]](#page-11-0). Since the 1990s, researchers such as Vagati, Staton, Matsuo, and Miller have conducted comprehensive studies to reduce torque ripple, improve saliency, and optimize barrier geometries [\[5](#page-11-0)–8]. FEM-based analyses have systematically investigated the effect of rotor barrier shapes, numbers, and angles on performance [[12,13,16,17](#page-11-0),[26\]](#page-11-0). It has also been shown that considering the current angle as a design parameter during SynRM rotor optimization improves torque density and reduces torque ripple [[31\]](#page-11-0). In application-specific studies, such as heating, ventilation, air conditioning (HVAC) applications and compressors [\[9\]](#page-11-0), rotor design was evaluated for its influence on overall performance. More recent strategies also account for assembly constraints and optimization requirements in EVs and railway applications [\[22](#page-11-0),[27,28,33,36](#page-11-0)–38].

In addition to SynRM's benefits, PMaSynRM offer enhanced torque density and power factor by integrating magnets into the rotor. This structure was first proposed as an alternative to IM for variable-speed drives [\[10](#page-11-0)]. Subsequent design approaches focused on minimizing magnet volume while maintaining performance [\[11](#page-11-0),[14,15\]](#page-11-0). The use of ferrite magnets, in particular, has attracted interest due to reduced dependency on rare earth elements and environmental considerations [18–[21\]](#page-11-0). Multidisciplinary analyses have been conducted to assess the effects of rotor shape, magnet placement, and barrier design on thermal, and mechanical performance [\[23,24](#page-11-0),[29,40\]](#page-11-0). A comparative analysis has further indicated that multiphase SynRM drive systems with and without permanent magnets involve significant cost, reliability, and performance trade-offs [\[39](#page-11-0)]. Furthermore, EV-focused PMaSynRM optimization studies have addressed torque ripple suppression, magnetic saturation modeling, and fault tolerance [\[25](#page-11-0),[32,34,35](#page-11-0)].

Despite these developments, there are limited studies in the literature addressing the direct convertibility of industrial IM platforms into SynRM or PMaSynRM using only rotor lamination changes. Although such an approach can offer significant advantages in terms of tooling cost, systematic investigations using existing industrial IM stators are rare. In addition, refurbishing methods have been proposed to convert existing three-phase SynRM stators into multiphase configurations at minimum cost, supporting the feasibility of low-cost industrial platform conversions [\[30](#page-11-0)]. Researchers such as Bianchi and Vagati have presented limited examples of compatibility between various rotor types and common stators, but comparative studies based on real industrial IM stators remain insufficient.

The long-term goal of this study is to adapt the developed motor for EV use. However, the initial objective focuses on enabling a costeffective transition within industrial applications by maintaining the

existing IM infrastructure. In this context, the stator design of a reference IE2-class IM was retained while developing SynRM and PMaSynRM models compatible with it. Their electromagnetic performance was analyzed, and a dimensional comparison was also made with the IE4 version of the same motor. Prototypes of the IMs were manufactured, and experimental data were collected to validate simulation-based comparisons. Only IM prototypes were produced at this stage, yet the test data will provide a foundation for structural and performance comparisons with future synchronous motors. The proposed motor designs are also directly applicable to widely used industrial systems such as fans, pumps, and HVAC, enhancing the study's practical relevance. This approach offers a scalable and cost-efficient pathway to adopting high-efficiency motor technologies without requiring major changes in manufacturing infrastructure.

## *1.1. Research gaps*

The reviewed literature demonstrates significant methodological progress in electromagnetic modeling, rotor geometry optimization, and performance evaluation. However, most studies remain limited in scope; they typically focus on specific design aspects or application scenarios while neglecting manufacturability and long-term reliability considerations. Overall, the literature provides strong advances in electromagnetic design, but the transition costs from IM platforms to SynRM or PMaSynRM configurations and the related issue of industrial scalability have not been sufficiently addressed. This shortcoming defines the research gap targeted in the present study.

#### *1.2. Problem statement*

Significant progress has been made in SynRM and PMaSynRM research; however, systematic investigations into the direct conversion of existing industrial IM platforms into high-efficiency synchronous machines remain insufficient. Most reported studies either introduce entirely new designs or rely on academic prototypes, thereby neglecting manufacturability, tooling costs, and large-scale industrial applicability. This gap creates a clear disconnect between academic findings and industrial requirements. To bridge this gap, the stator design of an existing IE2-class IM has been retained, and a common rotor structure compatible with both SynRM and PMaSynRM configurations has been developed. In this way, two different motor topologies can be realized with minimal transition cost, without the need for new stator or winding tooling. The problem addressed in this study is therefore the development of cost-effective, scalable, and industrially compatible motor topologies capable of achieving higher efficiency classes (IE4/IE5).

## **2. Design and analysis methodology**

## *2.1. Reference motor and design approach*

In this study, to minimize production tooling costs and maximize the utilization of the existing manufacturing infrastructure, the stator structure of a 90-frame, 4-pole, 1.5 kW squirrel-cage IM—currently in series production by Gamak—was selected as the design basis. This stator has 36 slots and is designed to operate at a nominal voltage of 400 V and a frequency of 50 Hz. As the equivalent reference motor, the AGM2EL 90 L 4b model—classified under the IE2 efficiency class and featuring an 80 mm stack length—was chosen. With an efficiency rating of 81.5 %, this motor served as the primary reference in both numerical comparisons and prototyping activities. Prototype production of both the IE2 and IE4 versions of the 90-frame reference motor was completed, allowing electromagnetic analyses to be validated against physical test data and enhancing the practical applicability of the study.

The SynRM design was developed based on this fixed stator platform. The PMaSynRM configuration was derived by integrating permanent magnets into the same rotor geometry, thus establishing a structurally

compatible alternative architecture. Through rotor lamination optimizations, it became possible to operate different motor types on the same stator. This approach is based on the principle of "Efficiency Class Upgrade with Minimum Tooling Cost," whereby higher-efficiency motor designs were realized at low cost through rotor topology optimization without modifying the existing tooling infrastructure.

The design process was carried out using MOGA-based optimization method. Conflicting performance criteria such as torque production, efficiency, magnet volume, and torque ripple were considered to generate Pareto-optimal solutions. Rotor geometries were analyzed in detail using the FEA. As a result, structurally compatible and electromagnetically optimized SynRM alternatives were presented—not only at the theoretical level, but also in a way that is practically integrable into production.

#### *2.2. Preliminary design work*

Before defining the motor's fundamental geometric parameters and design constraints, a comprehensive preliminary design phase was conducted for the rotor structure with an 80 mm stack length. In this stage, the following parameters were treated as variables, and their effects on motor performance were investigated through parametric analyses: Rotor position, control angel, W (bridge thickness), B0 (barrier width), H (distance of flux barriers from the rotor outer diameter, rib thickness), R0 (barrier sharpness), R1 (tip radius of the barrier), Rb (starting position of the first barrier).

The effects of these parameters on torque, torque ripple, output power, iron losses, efficiency, and power factor were investigated, and optimal values were identified within specific ranges. The SynRM profile was designed with a four-flux-barrier structure, aiming to achieve a high Ld/Lq ratio.

## *2.3. Rotor optimization: MOGA*

Following the preliminary design phase, MOGA-based approach was employed for rotor optimization. For this purpose, the mathematical model of the four objective functions was defined as follows.

- *g*1: *ηxT*→ Maximized
- *g*2: *η/TR*→ Maximized
- *g*3: *ηxT/TR*→ Maximized
- Preliminary design conditional performance criteria: *η >* 89*.*98%, *T >* 9*.*55*Nm*, *TR <* %10.

Here, η denotes efficiency, *T* torque, and *TR* torque ripple. In addition, rotor parameters (W, H, B0, R0, etc.) were defined as optimization variables and evaluated through FEA.

Each of the above functions was used during the optimization process to balance conflicting objectives while also considering manufacturability. By employing the MOGA, Pareto-optimal solutions were obtained that simultaneously optimize different performance criteria.

Fig. 1 provides a holistic summary of the overall optimization workflow. The process begins with the fixed stator platform and problem constraints (frame, slotting and winding configuration, manufacturability rules, etc.) as described in [Section 2.1](#page-1-0). Based on these inputs, preliminary parametric scans (Section 2.2) are carried out; using 2D FEA, critical rotor features (e.g., barrier width, bridge thickness, etc.) are swept to establish feasible regions and sensitivity trends. The resulting insights provide the starting conditions for the MOGA-based multi-objective rotor optimization (Section 2.3); the design space is systematically explored under the stated constraints to increase torque density, efficiency, and power factor while reducing torque ripple. In the final stage, benchmarking and selection criteria (Section 2.4) are applied: Pareto-efficient candidates are re-verified, compared across IM, SynRM, and PMaSynRM on the common stator, and filtered and evaluated in terms of performance breakdowns. This workflow links design-

## *3.2. Optimization results*

This optimization study was primarily conducted for the SynRM configuration. In the design of PMaSynRM, the key determinant of the rotor geometry is the reluctance component, while the magnets are integrated into this structure afterward. Therefore, the rotor geometry was first optimized for the magnet-free SynRM to maximize reluctance torque production.

Based on the optimal rotor geometry obtained for the SynRM, magnet placement will be performed in the subsequent phase to develop the PMaSynRM design. This approach enables a systematic treatment of the magnet integration process, which is the fundamental distinction between the two motor types.

MOGA-based optimization approach was adopted for rotor design. In this study, three objective functions based on electromagnetic performance criteria were defined, and additional filtering was applied based on combinations that satisfy specific conditional constraints.

#### • Efficiency × Torque → Maximized

The goal is to achieve both high energy efficiency and sufficient torque production. This product represents the motor's overall performance under load conditions.

#### • Efficiency / Torque Ripple → Maximized

The ratio of efficiency to torque ripple is important for the motor's stability and vibration-free operation. The higher this ratio, the quieter and smoother the motor runs.

## • (Efficiency × Torque) / Torque Ripple → Maximized

This composite function combines three parameters to evaluate the **Fig. 2.** Geometric parameters of the rotor. overall electromechanical efficiency of the system, taking torque imbalance into account.

• Conditional Constraints: Efficiency *>* 84.98 %, Torque *>* 9.55 Nm, Torque Ripple *<* 10 %

Upon examining the performance results obtained from the MOGA optimizations, the 8th design was found to offer a well-balanced structure in terms of efficiency, torque, and torque ripple. With the bridge thickness limited to 0.5 mm, this design presented an acceptable rotor configuration for the intended application. The rib structure located along the outer periphery of the rotor plays a critical role in both the electromagnetic and mechanical performance of the motor. The thickness in this region, represented by the H parameter, is a key design criterion to prevent localized magnetic saturation and to enhance mechanical strength at high speeds. Therefore, based on the selected 8th design, a final precision optimization study was conducted by varying only the H parameter to produce a design more suitable for mass production conditions and more tolerant to manufacturing deviations. Two different variants were analyzed (H = 1.0 mm and H = 1.5 mm). As a result of this analysis, the 9th optimization corresponding to H = 1.0 mm yielded similar electromagnetic performance while providing a mechanically more robust structure.

Consequently, considering performance criteria such as efficiency, torque, torque ripple, output power, and power factor—alongside manufacturability, mechanical integrity, and tolerance sensitivity—the rotor parameters of the 9th design were selected as the final prototype and mass production configuration. ### *3.3.1. Types of magnets used*

A comparative analysis was conducted using two different types of magnets in the designs:

- 1. NdFeB (Neodymium Iron Boron):
- o These magnets possess high energy density and can generate stronger magnetic flux within a smaller volume.
- o They were preferred in scenarios targeting high performance.
- 2. Ferrite Magnets:
- o Although they have lower energy density, they were considered for cost-effective applications due to their low cost and high thermal resistance.
- o They were used in price-performance-oriented scenarios.

For both magnet types, the total magnet volume was limited to no more than 12 % of the total rotor volume. The magnets were selected to be compatible with the depth and thickness of the rotor barriers.

# *3.4.2. Testing process and comparison*

## **4. Discussion**

In this section, the outcomes of the design and analysis studies are comparatively evaluated alongside the test results. The findings are discussed in the context of the performance of three different motor types: the IM, the SynRM, and the PMaSynRM. By preserving the same stator structure and altering only the rotor design, the advantages, limitations, and application potentials of these alternative motor topologies are examined. In addition, the proposed optimization method controls the overall system behavior by directly linking rotor design variables to performance indicators. Barrier width, bridge thickness, and other rotor design parameters, together with magnet placement, determine the flux distribution in the machine, which directly affects torque production, efficiency, torque ripple, and power factor. Within the MOGA framework, these variables were systematically varied and evaluated through FEA. As a result, system-level responses such as minimizing ripple or maximizing efficiency have become controllable through design-level decisions.

Finally, to position our contribution within the broader literature, we note the following: Compared to state-of-the-art approaches in the literature—which often propose entirely new machine structures or rely on academic prototypes—the proposed method offers several distinctive advantages. By preserving the existing IM stator platform, die/tooling costs are eliminated and large-scale industrial compatibility is ensured. Within a MOGA-based optimization framework, rotor parameters are tuned to simultaneously balance torque density, efficiency, torque ripple, and power factor, whereas many prior studies treat these objectives in isolation. Moreover, SynRM and PMaSynRM configurations are realized on a common rotor structure, enabling modularity and costeffective adaptability. These features highlight the originality and practical impact of the proposed approach compared with conventional methodologies.

## *4.3. Material and manufacturing impacts*

In this study, all motor configurations were designed using different rotor topologies and lamination materials while maintaining the same stator and winding structure. This approach enabled the development of various motor technologies (IM, SynRM, and PMaSynRM) with only rotor modifications, without altering the existing production infrastructure. As a result, mold costs were minimized, and significant advantages were achieved in terms of manufacturing flexibility and scalability.

From the perspective of lamination materials, the use of low-loss M400 steel led to improvements in efficiency, power factor, and torque smoothness for both SynRM and PMaSynRM motors. This outcome highlights once again the critical role of lamination quality, especially in applications targeting high-efficiency classes.

In terms of rotor manufacturing, SynRM designs require precise implementation of flux barrier geometries in the laminations. In PMa-SynRM motors, additional manufacturing steps are needed for magnet placement, orientation, and fixation. The proper positioning and insulation of ferrite magnets, in particular, demand careful attention. Configurations using NdFeB magnets require even more stringent control, both in magnet assembly and thermal management.

Despite these manufacturing differences, the variety achieved through a shared stator platform clearly demonstrates the value of modular design and production standardization in electric motor development processes.

#### *4.4. Variable frequency drive compatibility and application areas*

SynRM and PMaSynRM motors, by design, belong to the class of synchronous machines that are not suitable for direct grid connection but are operated via a drive (VFD). When combined with benefits such as high efficiency, low torque ripple, and a wide speed control range, their compatibility with variable frequency drives enables these motor types to stand out in various industrial applications.

PMaSynRM motors, thanks to their magnet-assisted structures, offer higher power factor and efficiency compared to conventional SynRM motors, enabling more efficient use of current drawn from the drive. Furthermore, the absence of copper or aluminum conductors in their rotors minimizes rotor losses and provides thermal advantages. In particular, PMaSynRM motors with NdFeB magnets stand out in highperformance applications—most notably in EVs—due to their high torque density, high efficiency, and low losses. The lack of rotor conduction losses in these structures offers potential for simplified thermal management systems, while proper magnet placement can expand the safe operating limits of the motor.

On the other hand, magnet-free SynRM motors are emerging as costeffective alternatives for industrial applications that seek to avoid rare earth materials. These motors attract attention in drive-controlled applications such as fans, pumps, and compressors, offering high efficiency and stable torque production.

## **5. Conclusions**

In this study, three different motor types—IM, SynRM, and PMa-SynRM—were developed by altering only the rotor topology while using a common industrial stator platform. This modular design approach, which maintains the same stator lamination, stack length, and winding structure, it has become possible to manufacture three distinct motor products on a single production line. We adopted a rotor-only, commonstator pathway combined with a MOGA-driven FEA workflow. This preserves manufacturability and ensures directly comparable evaluations among IM, SynRM, and PMaSynRM within the same package. The methodology yielded explicit trade-off maps (torque density, efficiency, torque ripple, and power factor) and robust rotor solutions, while retaining the existing stator and winding tooling.

Eight different motor configurations were analyzed comparatively, considering various lamination materials (M700 and M400) and magnet usage scenarios. The performance parameters were evaluated in detail, and the key findings can be summarized as follows:

- PMaSynRM motors, particularly those using NdFeB magnets, achieved the highest efficiency (IE5 class, 90.25 %) and power factor (*>*0.89). This configuration is highly suitable for high-performance applications and EVs.
- Ferrite magnet-based PMaSynRM configurations offered a compelling alternative for cost-sensitive EV platforms and industrial applications by delivering reasonable performance without the use of rare earth elements.
- SynRM motors, despite being magnet-free, demonstrated environmentally friendly and sustainable performance through their low torque ripple and high efficiency.
- IMs remain relevant, particularly due to their compatibility with direct line starting and ease of integration into existing systems.
- The use of M400-grade steel had a positive impact on the efficiency and performance of all motor types.

Practical implications from these results:

- Preserving the common stator enables rotor-only redesigns to upgrade the efficiency class; no new stator/winding tooling is required, so upfront investment is reduced and time-to-market is shortened.
- A common stator architecture simplifies inventory management, reduces operating costs, streamlines sourcing, and supports modular product families (IM ↔ SynRM ↔ PMaSynRM).

We also considered alternatives such as a full stator redesign and

changes to the winding scheme. However, these options were not adopted because they (i) undermine cross-topology comparability, (ii) increase development time/cost and require additional tooling changes, and (iii) do not adequately capture the multi-objective trade-offs central to this study.

In thermal analyses, the temperature rise of the IE2 IM was used as a reference. Lower nominal operating temperatures were projected for the SynRM and PMaSynRM configurations, due to the absence of rotor conduction losses and their higher load-handling capacity. Additionally, friction losses caused by the synchronous operation of SynRM and PMaSynRM motors were revised and included in the evaluation.

From an audience perspective, this work will be of primary interest to motor OEMs, drive/inverter manufacturers, and application engineers seeking to migrate existing IM product lines to SynRM/PMaSynRM with minimal tooling change. For researchers and practitioners, it provides a replicable rotor-only pathway on a common stator that makes the trade-offs among torque density, efficiency, torque ripple, and power factor explicit. The contributions are long-term, aligned with tightening efficiency regulations and efforts to reduce reliance on rare-earth materials, since preserving the common stator with a rotor-only approach allows the same rotor design to operate as magnet-free SynRM and, when required, as a magnet-assisted PMaSynRM. Moreover, within the same common-stator framework, the method can be generalized to motors with different pole counts through straightforward reparameterization.

In conclusion, this study provides a strong foundation for developing modular, scalable, and manufacturable motor architectures not only for current industrial systems but also for future EV applications. The ability to derive three different motor types from a single stator platform represents a significant engineering achievement in terms of costeffectiveness, design flexibility, and ease of series production.
